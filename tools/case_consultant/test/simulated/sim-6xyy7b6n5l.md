## Read-back (approved by the expert 2026-10-09)

You are the senior buyer at a regional used construction equipment dealer, fourteen years in. The decision is which machines to bid on at online auctions and whether to pay for an independent inspection first, bid without one, or pass. It comes up a few times a month, and the call and the max bid are yours. You are after margin per machine once hammer price, the 10% premium, transport, repairs and any inspection come out, with the cash back inside about 90 days. A winning bid is binding: backing out forfeits the 10% deposit and gets you banned, which you say is worse than the money.

Others act around you. The owner sets the inventory cap of about $400,000 and looks at quarterly gross margin, not single machines. The auction house writes the listing, knows the seller's history and won't volunteer problems. Sellers want the machine gone; many are fleets clearing out or contractors in trouble. Other dealers want the same as you; contractors often go past your max because they will run the machine, and you lose machines that way pretty often, with no count. The inspector protects his name, so he leans cautious, especially on undercarriages. Your mechanic goes through the machine in the first couple of weeks and gives you the repair list.

You see photos, the hour meter, the year, the seller's condition note, an auction condition score you barely trust, and service records when there are any. You cannot see the hydraulic pump, the final drives, or whether the hour meter is honest; that mostly comes out only in your yard. From your log of roughly 400 machines, about four in ten with no records or an unknown seller have a major hidden fault, against about one in ten for a fleet seller with records. High hours push that up a bit, but you have not split it out.

Before bidding you can call the auction rep (free, about twenty minutes, rarely useful) or ask for records through the auction house (free, about a day, they come about half the time). You can pay the inspector, usually around $550 ($450 to $700), two working days at the minimum, twice a week at most, and not at all with two days left or less. You can add an oil sample for $90 and three more days. The inspection catches about seven in ten real problems, misses mostly intermittent hydraulic faults, and wrongly flags about one in ten good machines. The oil sample catches maybe one more in ten. If the inspector flags hydraulics or anything that smells major, you are out; if it is only undercarriage wear, you may bid lower by the cost of that wear.

You count resale after cleanup, minus hammer, premium, transport ($1,800 to $3,500, usually $2,400), repairs and inspection, plus about 9% a year on money tied up. A sound machine nets $8,000 to $12,000 and sells in three to six weeks. A bad one usually means about $20,000 of repairs ($15,000 to $30,000), sits two to three months and loses $8,000 to $10,000. A slow machine also uses up room under the cap, which you did not put a figure on. Passing costs nothing directly, or $550 after an inspection, but passing too often leaves the yard light. You judge over a bunch of machines, not one.

What makes it hard is that inspecting costs $550 and two days and loses quick deals, while not inspecting risks the hidden fault. Your rule: fleet seller with records and under about $45,000, bid without inspecting, up to the lower of 75% of resale and resale minus $12,000 minus expected repairs. Over $50,000, or no records, inspect or pass. Between $45,000 and $50,000 it is a judgment call, and about three cases in ten sit near the line. Days left can force it, since you often notice a listing with three days to go. Hours (3,000 to 12,000) and freight do not flip the inspect call; freight lowers your max and can tip you to pass.

Someone making no effort bids on whatever looks cheap against book and loses money on about a third. A capable newcomer trusts the condition score and photos and bids up to book less 10%, which works about six times in ten and fails on machines without records. You set your number and stick to it; your own usual mistake is going past it in the last minutes, which has cost a few thousand more than once. With everything hidden in view you guess you would make maybe $6,000 more per machine.

Becomes: The seat being tested, and what it is scored on.

- **The decision, in one sentence:** Which machines to bid on at auction, and whether to check them out first (a winning bid is binding)

**Options on the table**

- Pay for an independent inspection first, then bid
- Bid blind, without checking
- **What they are trying to achieve:** Margin per machine after everything comes out (hammer price, premium, transport, repairs, inspection), with the cash back within about 90 days so the machine does not sit. A sound machine that sells in a few weeks with a decent margin is a good buy; one sitting in the yard for months is a bad one, even if the repair bill looks survivable.

## Tension

Becomes: The hard cases: worlds on each side of the break-even.

- **What pulls each way:** Inspecting costs about $550 and two days, and he loses quick deals if he inspects everything. Not inspecting risks a major hidden fault: about one in ten with a fleet seller and records, about four in ten with no records or an unknown seller.
- **What would flip the choice:** A fleet seller with full service records and a price under about $45,000: he would bid without inspecting. Timing can also force it: with two days left he cannot book the inspector, so he has to pass or bid blind.
- **The level at which it flips:** Under about $45,000 with a fleet seller and full records, he bids without inspecting. Over $50,000 he always inspects or passes. Between $45,000 and $50,000 it is a judgment call, not a clean rule: a contractor with complete records at $48,000 he would have bid on without inspecting, with only partial records he passed. The last case was a contractor, no records, over $50,000, so inspecting was the only sensible call.
- **Cases of ten that sit near the line:** About three cases in ten sit near that line.

## Yardsticks and typical mistakes

Becomes: Reference players (no effort, newcomer rule, professional rule, full knowledge) and failure labels.

- **No effort: the default, and how it turns out:** Bid on whatever looks cheap against book value. Loses money on about a third of them (his rough feel, not counted).
- **What a capable newcomer does:** A capable newcomer trusts the condition score and the photos, and bids up to book less 10%.
- **Where that goes wrong, and how often it still works:** Works about six times in ten. It goes wrong on machines without records, because that is where the hidden faults are.
- **The professional's rule:** Fleet seller with records and under $45,000: bid up to 75% of resale without inspecting. Over $50,000, or no records: inspect or pass. Never bid blind, which to him means no inspection on a no-records machine or on anything over $50,000; a fleet machine with records is not blind, because the records are the check. Max bid is the hammer price alone: resale, minus $12,000 (a rough allowance for premium, freight and margin, not exact), minus expected repairs. Without an inspection he uses the lower of the 75% cap and that figure. Then stick to it in the last minutes.
- **With everything hidden in view:** Would only buy the sound ones and walk away from the rest. Would bid harder on the good ones, because no cushion would be needed for the machine that turns out bad. No inspections, so he would save the $550 and the two days and lose fewer quick deals.
- **Money between newcomer and professional:** Maybe $6,000 more per machine with everything hidden in view, but that is a guess; he has not worked it out properly. Most of it comes from not eating the $20,000 repair jobs.
