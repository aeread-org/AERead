## Read-back (approved by the expert 2026-10-15)

You buy used construction machines at online auctions and resell them to contractors. Your job is to decide which listings to chase and bid on, and how much to bid, with the machine far away and often only a few days left. You bid personally, and the owner only watches the quarterly gross margin inside a capped inventory.

What you can see at the start: photos, the hour meter reading, the seller's condition note, the auction house's condition score, the year, and whether the seller is a fleet or a contractor. Service records are available about half the time when asked, and take about a day. About 4 in 10 listings have records. Hours usually run about 3,000 to 12,000.

What is hidden: the true condition of the machine, mainly hydraulic pump, final drive, undercarriage and whether the hour meter was swapped. About 4 in 10 no-records machines have a major fault, from your purchase log of about 400 machines. About 1 in 10 fleet machines with records have one. The auction house and the seller will not volunteer problems.

Your moves: a pre-bid inspection costs about 550, usually 450 to 700, and takes a couple of working days; the inspector is shared, about two a week. It catches about 7 in 10 major hidden faults and flags about 1 in 10 good machines wrongly; it misses about 3 in 10. An oil sample adds about 90 and three days and catches about one more fault in ten. A winning bid is binding: the buyer's premium is 10 percent of hammer price, and backing out loses the 10 percent deposit and the bidding ban. Transport is about 2,400, from 1,800 to 3,500 by distance.

How the result is counted: a sound machine, after premium, transport, repairs and inspection, makes about 8,000 to 12,000, with about 3,000 of repairs. A bad machine usually loses about 8,000 to 10,000 whatever the price; repairs of 15,000 to 30,000 move it most. Carrying cost on money tied up is about 9 percent a year, roughly 1,000 to 2,000 per machine. Cash should not sit more than about 90 days, because stuck cash cannot go into the next machine.

What makes it hard: inspecting costs a few hundred dollars and a couple of days, and can lose quick deals; skipping risks a bad buy. The rule turns on price and seller type: a fleet seller with proper records under about 45,000 you bid without inspecting; over 50,000, or with no records, you inspect or pass and never bid blind. Between about 45,000 and 50,000 is the gray zone, about 3 in 10 listings by feel, where you sometimes cannot tell.

A newcomer who bids on the auction score and photos, up to book value less about 10 percent, is right about six times in ten, and fails on no-records machines and on forgetting premium and freight. Your professional rule: for a fleet seller with records under about 45,000, bid up to roughly 75 percent of expected resale; over 50,000 or with no records, inspect or pass; the top bid is the lower of 75 percent of expected resale and expected resale minus about 12,000 minus expected repairs. With no records or partial records and no inspector available, you pass. This is judgment held for years and never tested against the log.
