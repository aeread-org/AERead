THE FACTS (true; use them, never contradict them except as told above)
The decision comes up about three times a month.
Last time: a 2017 20-ton excavator, 6,200 hours on the meter, no service records, seller was a contractor. You expected it to go for about $62,000. Resale after cleanup about $78,000. You paid for an inspection, it came back clean, you bid and won at $60,500.
Goal: margin per machine after repairs, without cash sitting in a machine for more than 90 days. The owner judges you on quarterly gross margin.
Others involved: the auction house (wants a high hammer price and volume, writes the listing, knows the seller's history, will not volunteer problems). The seller (wants it gone, knows the maintenance history, often a fleet clearing out or a contractor in trouble). Rival bidders, other dealers and contractors, three to eight active on a machine (a guess). Contractors bid higher because they will use the machine themselves.
What you see: listing photos, hour meter, year, a condition note written by the seller, the auction house's own condition score from 1 to 5 which you barely trust, and service records on about 4 listings in 10.
What you cannot see: the state of the hydraulic pump and final drives, undercarriage wear beyond the photos, whether the hour meter was swapped. It comes out in the first two weeks in your yard, when your mechanic goes through the machine.
Signs: a fleet seller with service records has a major hidden fault about 1 time in 10. No records or an unknown seller, about 4 in 10. Fresh paint on a high-hour machine is a warning. These come from your own purchase log of roughly 400 machines.
What you can do before bidding: call the auction rep (free, twenty minutes, rarely tells you much). Ask for service records (free, a day, you get them about half the time). Pay an independent inspector: $450 to $700, usually $550, from invoices; needs two working days to schedule and must happen before the auction closes. He cannot open the hydraulics but does a pressure test. An oil sample is $90 extra and the lab takes three more days.
Committing: a winning bid is binding. Backing out forfeits a 10% deposit and gets you banned. There is a 10% buyer's premium on top of the hammer price. Transport is $1,800 to $3,500, usually $2,400.
Limits: listings open about seven days before close, and you often only notice one with three days left. The inspector is shared, so you get at most two inspections a week. The dealer caps inventory at about $400,000.
What the inspection tells you: it catches about 7 major hidden faults in 10; it misses intermittent hydraulic problems. It wrongly flags about 1 good machine in 10 because the inspector is cautious about undercarriages. The oil sample catches maybe one more of the ten. Sellers can hide a fault for a short test by topping up with heavy oil. These rates are your experience, not a log.
The money: margin is resale minus hammer price, the 10% premium, transport, repairs and the inspection. A sound machine needs $2,000 to $5,000 of repairs, usually $3,000, and makes $8,000 to $12,000. A machine with a major hidden fault needs $15,000 to $30,000, usually $20,000, and loses $8,000 to $10,000. A sound one sells in three to six weeks, a bad one sits two to three months.
What makes it hard: the inspection costs money and two days, and you can lose the machine to a faster bidder. You skip the inspection when the seller is a fleet with records and the machine is under about $45,000. You inspect, or pass, on anything over $50,000 or without records. If the last excavator had come from a fleet with full records and been under $45,000 you would have bid without inspecting. About 3 cases in 10 sit near that line.
How cases differ: price $25,000 to $140,000, most between $40,000 and $80,000. Hours 3,000 to 12,000. Records on 4 in 10. Seller is a fleet 5 in 10, a contractor 3 in 10, unknown 2 in 10. Days left when you notice it, one to seven. Fleet sellers mostly have records. Cheaper machines tend to have higher hours. The premium and the deposit rule never change.
Yardsticks: with no effort, bidding on whatever looks cheap against book value, you lose money on about a third. A capable newcomer trusts the condition score and the photos and bids up to book value less 10%. That works about 6 times in 10 and goes wrong on machines without records. Your rule: fleet seller with records and under $45,000, bid up to 75% of resale without inspecting; over $50,000 or no records, inspect or pass, never bid blind; maximum bid is resale minus $12,000 minus expected repairs. With everything in view you would buy only the sound ones and bid harder for them, worth maybe $6,000 more per machine (a guess). Newcomer against professional: about $4,000 a machine on average.
Mistakes: bidding past your limit in the last minutes, costs $3,000 to $5,000; trusting the condition score; forgetting transport on a distant machine; inspecting everything, which burns $550 a time and loses the quick deals.
Three situations. One: fleet seller, full records, $38,000, 5,000 hours, four days left. Bid without inspecting. Two: unknown seller, no records, $70,000 against a book value of $95,000, fresh paint, 9,000 hours, five days left. It looks like a bargain and bidding is the obvious move, but it is wrong: inspect. The real one had a pump whine and you passed. Three: contractor seller, partial records, $48,000, two days left so no inspection can be scheduled. Close call. You passed, and would have bid if the records had been complete.
Something nobody asks about: financing. The dealer pays about 9% a year on the money tied up in inventory, so a slow machine costs more than the repair bill.
