# Where the value is: GDP by economic activity, the categories, and the capability axes

What the national accounts can and cannot say about which economic activities carry
value, three lenses that decompose the economy by activity rather than by industry, a
category scheme with the capability axes each category tests, and procurement worked
through to the capability level with a current operating-model source.

**Status: a research note, 2026-10-08.** Every number is from a source in §7 and is
dated. The note produces no share of GDP for AERead; §6 says why none should be
claimed. Written for the owner's question "the value composition of GDP by economic
activities, the categories and the key capability axis, take procurement as an example".

---

## 1. The question

aeread.org now says the benchmark measures readiness for value delivery rather than
task completion. Choosing cases and weights for that claim needs an answer to a
question the accounts do not ask directly: when value changes hands in the economy,
what are the parties doing, and which capability decides the outcome? Industry shares
answer where value is booked. This note assembles what the official accounts do say,
adds three lenses that are organised by activity, proposes categories of activity with
their capability axes, and works one category, procurement, down to the level at which a
case can be written.

## 2. What the accounts measure (United States, 2025)

The 2025 annual figures, all from BEA through FRED [1-6]:

| Measure | 2025 | Share |
|---|---|---|
| GDP | $30.86T | |
| Personal consumption | $20.87T | 67.6% of GDP |
| Gross private domestic investment | $5.56T | 18.0% |
| Government consumption and gross investment | $5.36T | 17.4% |
| Net exports | -$0.92T | -3.0% |
| Compensation of employees (income side, average of quarters) | $15.70T | 50.9% |
| Gross output, all industries (average of quarters) | $52.80T | 171% |
| Intermediate inputs (gross output less GDP) | $21.94T | 71% of GDP, 42% of gross output |

Three readings matter for a benchmark.

- **The expenditure split is a split of buyers.** Households buy two thirds of final
  output, businesses invest a fifth, governments a sixth. Every dollar is a purchase
  decided by someone acting for themselves or for a principal.
- **Half of GDP is paid to people.** Compensation of employees is 51% of GDP. The hiring,
  pay and terms behind that figure are themselves negotiated transactions.
- **GDP nets out the largest flow of all.** Businesses bought $21.9 trillion of goods and
  services from other businesses in 2025, 71% of GDP, and the accounts subtract it to
  avoid double counting. BEA's own definition: value added is "the difference between
  gross output and intermediate inputs", where intermediate inputs are "the foreign and
  domestically-produced goods and services used up by an industry in the process of
  producing its gross output" [6]. Each of those dollars was a procurement decision on
  one side and a sales decision on the other.

By industry, the value-added shares BEA reported for Q2 2024 (the table GDPval used to
pick its sectors [7]) are: real estate and rental 13.8%, government 11.3%, manufacturing
10.0%, professional, scientific and technical services 8.1%, health care 7.6%, finance and
insurance 7.4%, retail trade 6.3%, wholesale trade 5.8%, information 5.4%. These say where
value is recorded. Procurement, selling, financing, hiring and contracting happen inside
every one of them, which is why an activity lens is needed at all.

## 3. Three lenses organised by activity

**The transaction sector.** Wallis and North measured the resources the American economy
devotes to transacting, meaning search, bargaining, contracting, enforcement and the
information work around them, as opposed to transforming inputs. It was 25% of GNP in
1870 and 45% by 1970 [8]. No official series continues the measure, and the sectors they
counted (finance, law, wholesale and retail, management) have grown since. The
conservative statement is that close to half of economic activity, measured a generation
ago, was the activity of making exchanges happen.

**Work time by activity.** McKinsey's 2016 decomposition of US work time across more than
2,000 activities and 800 occupations found managing others, applying expertise and
interfacing with stakeholders to take about 37% of all work time (7%, 14% and 16%), and
to be the activities with the lowest technical automation potential at 9%, 18% and 20%
[9]. Data collection and processing, about a third of time, sat at 64-69%. The activities
that decide outcomes are the ones task-completion technology reaches last, which is the
gap the site's framing names.

**Purchasing flows.** Besides the $21.9T of intermediate purchases, private fixed
investment was $5.6T in 2025 [3] and general-government procurement was 10.1% of GDP in
2024, against an OECD average of 12.9% [10]. At the firm level, third-party spend
"represents the biggest cost element as a percentage of revenue for most companies,
averaging more than 60 percent in manufacturing and 30 percent in services industries"
[11]. Purchasing decisions govern a flow of the same order as GDP itself without being
GDP, the clearest case of why §6 refuses to add shares.

## 4. Categories of economic activity and their capability axes

The categories below are activities in which value changes hands through interaction with
other actors. The capability axes use the five mechanisms in the owner's framing note:
search and matching, complementarity and structuring, bargaining under private
information, commitment and coordination, and principal protection and refusal. The last
column is where AERead already has cases; it is a coverage map, not a weight.

| Activity | Scale proxy (source) | Counterparties | Where value is won or lost | Capability axes | AERead coverage |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Procurement and sourcing | $21.9T intermediate inputs; public procurement 10.1% of GDP; 30-60%+ of revenue [1,6,10,11] | suppliers, requesters, approvers, risk functions | supplier choice, price and terms, award allocation, compliance, risk status | search and matching; bargaining under private information; structuring; commitment; protection and refusal | procurement grounding, allocation, information-decision pilot, repeated sourcing |
| Sales and distribution | wholesale 5.8% + retail 6.3% of value added [7] | buyers, channels, platforms | pricing, discounts, terms, returns, service levels | bargaining; matching; commitment; margin protection | exchange cases (bilateral, clearing), bundle-purchase worlds, refund and return tasks |
| Financing and capital allocation | finance and insurance 7.4% of value added; investment 18.0% of GDP [3,7] | lenders, investors, partners | valuation, structure, risk allocation, covenants, when to decline | structuring; bargaining; contingent commitment; refusal | data-center risk allocation, joint venture and tender cases; TERMS-Bench |
| Hiring and labor contracting | compensation 50.9% of GDP [5] | candidates, employers, intermediaries | matching, pay and terms, retention | matching; bargaining under private information; commitment | none |
| Real estate market transactions | real estate 13.8% of value added [7] | tenants and landlords, buyers and sellers, brokers | price, who gets what under private preferences, adverse selection | matching; bargaining; consent; protection | housing cases: search and signing, lemons, price bargaining, outside demand |
| Contracting, compliance and dispute | lawyers are a $137B occupation in GDPval's own table [7]; contracts underlie every row | counterparties, regulators, courts | terms, enforcement, remedies, refunds | commitment and coordination; protection and refusal | refund and return tasks; consent cases; TERMS-Bench critical-violation scoring |
| Coordination and logistics | manufacturing 10.0%, wholesale 5.8% of value added [7] | many parties at once | joint feasibility, timing, deferred settlement | complementarity and structuring; commitment and coordination | clearing and deferred-settlement cases; bundle worlds |
| Public allocation and regulation | government 11.3% of value added; procurement 10.1% of GDP [7,10] | agencies, bidders, the public | tenders, compliance, commons | bargaining under rules; refusal | tender cases; GovSim (commons governance) |

Two readings. Coverage is deepest where the repository started, in procurement, exchange,
real estate and contract terms; hiring and most of financing are open. And the axes
recur: bargaining under private information appears in six of eight rows, protection and
refusal in six. A benchmark organised by mechanism covers more of the table than one
organised by industry.

## 5. Procurement, worked through

### 5.1 Scale

$21.9T of intermediate purchases in 2025 [1,6]; general-government procurement at 10.1% of
GDP [10]; third-party spend above 60% of revenue in manufacturing and about 30% in
services [11]. Kearney adds that fewer than 10% of companies "leverage this capability to
its fullest extent" and that leaders in third-party economics generate nearly twice the
shareholder returns [11].

### 5.2 The operating model (Zip and KPMG, October 2026)

The piece the owner pointed to [12] describes procurement moving "from process execution
to commercial architecture", with decision rights "enforced at the point where the
decision happens, inside the workflow". Its division of labour is a ready-made taxonomy:

- **Agents settle:** gathering requirements, comparing suppliers, drafting, routing;
  renewals within agreed terms, below thresholds, with approved suppliers; catalog
  replenishment straight through.
- **Escalated to people:** price increases above category limits; suppliers whose risk
  status changed; "complex negotiations and market-shaping commercial strategy";
  "ambiguous multi-year statements of work with non-standard indemnities".
- **Owned by category leaders:** the boundaries themselves, updated as "supplier
  conditions, commercial priorities and market dynamics change".

Its numbers: agent orchestration doubled from 9% to 18% of organisations in one quarter
(KPMG AI Quarterly Pulse, Q2 2026); 66% have monitoring dashboards and 61% approval
processes, but only 26% full real-time visibility of AI cost; in one industrial case,
manual touchpoints fell 65% after governance was put in place, while the legacy thresholds
had produced false-positive escalations on low-dollar items and let non-standard contracts
bypass review. The authors say the value metrics shift from cycle time, requests completed,
savings captured and spend under management to "which decisions an agent can settle on its
own" and the hours freed for supplier innovation and relationship structuring.

### 5.3 What that says about the value composition inside procurement

The operating model sorts procurement work into three tiers, and they carry value in
inverse proportion to their volume:

1. **Routine execution within thresholds.** High volume, little value at stake per
   decision, and the part already being automated (the 65% fewer touchpoints).
2. **The thresholds and rules.** Where the value of tier 1 is actually decided: a wrong
   limit either floods people with false positives or lets a bad contract through, as the
   case study shows.
3. **Complex negotiation, structuring and risk.** Low volume, most of the value at stake
   per decision, and explicitly reserved for people today.

Readiness for value delivery in procurement is therefore two things: how far into tier 3 an
agent's decisions can be trusted, and whether it knows which tier a decision belongs to,
which is the escalate-or-refuse capability the site's "constraint adherence" names.

### 5.4 Capability axes for procurement, as measurement targets

| Axis | What the agent must do | AERead case | Measured by |
|---|---|---|---|
| Evidence-grounded sourcing | find and qualify suppliers from evidence, not from the prompt | procurement grounding (frozen 231-project snapshot) | surplus against the optimum given the evidence |
| Bargaining under private information | price, lead time and terms against a supplier with its own reservation value | procurement allocation; information-decision pilot (when to buy information) | principal outcome against the outside option |
| Allocation and structuring under constraints | award across quality, lead time, landed cost, margin, return and refund terms; split or bundle | procurement allocation; bundle-purchase worlds | surplus realised; feasibility of the award |
| Commitment over the relationship | renewals, service levels, deferred performance, trust across rounds | repeated sourcing; deferred-settlement exchange case | outcome over the sequence, not one round |
| Protection and refusal | price above category limit, changed risk status, non-standard indemnity: escalate or walk away rather than close | consent cases; no-deal worlds; refund tasks | constraint adherence; abstention scored on its own |
| Decision-boundary awareness | know which decisions are the agent's to settle | not yet a case | share of decisions settled inside the mandate |

Each axis takes the framing note's three quantities: surplus realisation against a computed
optimum, the principal's outcome with the mandate respected, and the gain over a baseline
in matched worlds. The Zip and KPMG escalation list doubles as the refusal-trigger taxonomy
for the fifth axis, and the three tiers give a deployment distribution: weight by decision
frequency to answer the operational question, by value at stake to answer the economic one,
and report both, as the framing note requires.

## 6. What this does and does not license

- **Coverage claims, by mechanism.** "Performance across a stated set of interaction
  mechanisms", with the table in §4 as the map, is supportable now.
- **Deployment weights, from operating models.** Frequencies and value at stake per decision
  come from sources like §5.2, not from industry shares. Sales, financing, hiring and real
  estate each need a §5 of their own; that is the next research step.
- **No share of GDP.** The flows in §2 and §3 overlap by construction: intermediate inputs
  are not GDP, compensation is both income and the product of hiring, procurement recurs in
  every industry. Adding them, or attributing an activity's whole flow to the decisions in
  it, would count the same value several times. The owner's framing note already forbids
  "performance on X% of the economy"; this note gives the arithmetic reason.
- **The 1970 transaction-sector figure is a floor, not a current estimate**, and the
  McKinsey time shares describe time, not value.
