## Abstract

AI agents are increasingly asked to make economic decisions for a principal, such as whom to trade with, what to verify and when to commit. In these decisions a completed transaction can destroy value and a refusal can be correct, so task-completion or agreement scores cannot tell whether an agent served its principal. We present AERead, an environment that scores these decisions by the value an agent obtains. Each case defines a distribution of economic worlds, seats with private information, counterparts with their own interests, and a reference policy whose access to information is stated, so that a shortfall separates poor decisions from information the agent never had. One runtime executes every case and recomputes each score from the sealed record without calling a model, and quality control operates on populations of worlds rather than on single tasks. We build cases in housing, procurement, data-center contracting and refunds on this runtime. The counterpart is part of the measurement: the earnings gap between two agents is 42% of the value available in a deal when they negotiate against each other, and 7% when each faces the same scripted counterpart. We break shortfalls down by stratum and by action class; in housing, nearly all of the gap to the reference falls in worlds where the tenant’s favourite listing is a lemon. Of 25 defects selected from our quality-control record, 14 were invisible in any one world. Housing and Procurement now pass four of the five quality-control gates and no case passes all five, so our results compare named agents on stated worlds rather than rank models. AERead’s code is open source: a new case supplies its economic rules, worlds and reference, and inherits the runtime, replay and the quality-control standard.

## 1. Introduction

An agent buying on behalf of a firm must decide more than whether it can place an order. It must choose whom to trust, whether another test is worth its cost, and when to commit before an offer expires. A tenant faces the same problem when inspecting a home; a negotiator faces it when exchanging price for contractual risk. In these settings, completing a transaction can destroy value, while refusing it can be the right decision. We use *economic-agent readiness* to mean an agent’s ability to obtain value for a principal within a stated mandate, given limited information and other parties’ interests.

Evaluating this ability requires a reference for what the agent could have achieved. A good realized outcome can follow a poor decision, and a shortfall from a fully informed optimum can reflect information the agent never had. The counterpart also matters: a strategy that succeeds against a cooperative seller may fail against a different policy. Existing work provides interactive task evaluation and economic decision benchmarks (Merrill et al. 2026; Fish et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2026). The problem we address is how to make information, counterpart behavior and reference values explicit across different economic cases, then test whether the resulting scores measure the intended decisions.

AERead provides a framework for constructing and evaluating such cases. Each case specifies the parties, their observations and legal actions, the world distribution, and checks on behavior and outcomes. A shared runtime executes play and records evidence. Case-specific references give scores an economic interpretation, and results keep their native units.

AERead combines seven properties. Prior benchmarks have some of them, and TERMS-Bench has five in full. AERead has six in full and has the seventh, an inclusion criterion that screens out shortcuts, in three of its cases (Table 2.1). The first three properties concern a single world, the next two a case’s population of worlds, and the last two the runtime (Figure 1.1). We built each of them and applied it to the cases reported here.

1.  **Value against a reference with stated information.** Scores are economic values in each case’s own units, measured against a feasible policy, the best policy given the deciding seat’s information, or a labelled full-information bound. We play each reference through the environment on every included world (Section <a href="#sec:qc" data-reference="sec:qc" data-reference-type="ref">6</a>).

2.  **The counterpart is part of the case.** Other parties are seats with their own information and interests, played by scripted policies, another model or the same model, and each claim names its counterpart. In the Data-center case, the measured gap between two agents depends strongly on which counterpart they face (Section <a href="#sec:cases" data-reference="sec:cases" data-reference-type="ref">5</a>).

3.  **Typed shortfall.** The gap to the reference is split into observed action classes, such as tests not taken, worse terms, harmful agreements and negotiation fees, so a result states where value was lost as well as how much (Section <a href="#sec:cases" data-reference="sec:cases" data-reference-type="ref">5</a>).

4.  **World generators with an inclusion criterion.** A case is a generator of worlds, and its evaluation sets regenerate from their seeds. An inclusion criterion, computed before any agent plays, keeps only worlds in which simple shortcuts lose a stated margin. The Data-center comparison, for example, used 80 worlds included from 5,436 scanned (Section <a href="#sec:cases" data-reference="sec:cases" data-reference-type="ref">5</a>).

5.  **Quality control over populations of worlds.** Cases are qualified through quality-control gates (Section <a href="#sec:qc" data-reference="sec:qc" data-reference-type="ref">6</a>). We score simple-rule ceilings beside the agents and audit the task prompts. More than half of the defects we selected from our record were invisible in any single world.

6.  **Scores recomputed from sealed evidence.** The kernel hashes a plan before execution, seals the events, and replays each receipt to recompute its scores without calling a model; no model judges a score (Section <a href="#sec:runtime" data-reference="sec:runtime" data-reference-type="ref">4</a>).

7.  **One runtime for several activities.** Families supply only the economic rules. Four cases and a reimplementation of three TERMS-Bench counterpart families run on the same kernel (Section 5.3).

A researcher studying another economic decision therefore writes only its rules, worlds and reference. Execution, sealing, replay, counterpart seats and the quality-control gates come with the runtime, so a new case starts with the same evidence and checks as the existing ones. Sections 3 and 4 describe the case design and the runtime, Section 5 the cases, and Section 6 the quality control. Section 7 states what the results do and do not establish.

#### Economically valuable work.

GDPval selects professional tasks by occupation and evaluates deliverables through expert comparison (Patwardhan et al. 2025). Agents’ Last Exam (ALE) evaluates expert-authored professional workflows with task-specific verification (Sun et al. 2026). AERead evaluates decisions under a principal’s mandate, with responsive counterparts and payoff references that specify the deciding party’s information.

#### Interactive agent evaluation.

Terminal-Bench verifies final environment state and supplies human-written solutions (Merrill et al. 2026). AppWorld combines an application engine with state-based evaluation, including collateral-change checks (Trivedi et al. 2024). τ²-bench lets agent and user act on shared state (Barres et al. 2025).

#### Economic decisions and negotiation.

EconEvals covers procurement, scheduling, pricing and economic choice (Fish et al. 2025). NegotiationArena studies bargaining tactics (Bianchi et al. 2024); SidConArena combines negotiation, production and auctions (Feng, Chen, and He 2026). AgenticPay evaluates buyer–seller bargaining under private reservation values, scoring agreement, surplus allocation and speed (Liu, Gu, and Song 2026). TERMS-Bench specifies counterpart types, policies and payoffs to diagnose negotiation through oracle-reference gaps (Zhang et al. 2026); our case specifications follow its decomposition into environment, protocol and counterpart policy (Appendix A), and Section 5.3 reimplements three of its counterpart families on AERead’s runtime. AERead retains case-specific payoff units, distinguishes full-information bounds from information-constrained policy references, and applies quality control to world distributions and measurement paths.

#### Shared environments and evaluation quality.

OpenSpiel and PettingZoo provide common interfaces for games and multi-agent environments (Lanctot et al. 2019; Terry et al. 2021). AERead similarly separates shared execution from case semantics (Appendix H). Terminal-Bench combines executable reference solutions, automated checks, human review and adversarial exploit testing (Merrill et al. 2026). The Agentic Benchmark Checklist identifies task and reward defects that distort performance (Zhu et al. 2025). Our QC analysis tests whether prompts, controls and counterpart assignments support valid comparisons across distributions of economic worlds.

## 3. Task design and construction

Three of AERead’s properties are fixed when a case is designed: the reference and the information it may use, the counterpart seats, and the world generator with its inclusion criterion.

### 3.1 Cases, worlds and seats

A *case* defines an economic decision problem; a *world* is one instance of that design, with particular valuations, private types, costs and deadlines. A *seat* is a party’s position in the interaction, with its own objective, information and permitted actions. A policy occupies a seat and chooses actions from its observations and available history. The family implementation supplies the environment, observation rules, legal actions, transitions and verifier.

For example, Housing places a tenant among four listings with hidden quality. The tenant can inspect, make offers and decide whether to sign; landlord replies and outside demand affect the opportunities that remain. Inspection changes both information and cost. The verifier therefore needs to distinguish a legal action, a favorable realized outcome and a good decision given the tenant’s information.

In a controlled comparison, the evaluated policy occupies one seat and counterparts follow fixed policies. Cross-play and self-play change those assignments and answer different questions. Each comparison names the seat, counterpart configuration and world distribution to which it applies. Appendices A–D give the case specifications, counterpart policies, references and measures.

### 3.2 Selecting economic decisions

We organize coverage around activities in which a party’s choices affect the value of a transaction (Li 2026). A candidate case needs a consequential decision, a counterpart with its own interests and information, and an outcome that can be evaluated inside the world. The intended coverage spans business purchases, labour contracts, capital and corporate transactions, household purchases, and public purchases. Contractual terms, risk allocation and disputes enter as decisions within these transactions.

The four examples cover business and household purchases. They were selected to exercise different references and counterpart configurations; they are not a representative sample of economic activity. Economic stakes motivate which decisions to study, but the reported scores are case-specific quantities, without a cross-family aggregate or an estimate of economy-wide gains.

### 3.3 Constructing and qualifying a case

A contribution begins with a decision and an interpretation of success. The case author specifies the principal’s objective and constraints, what each seat can observe, which actions commit resources, and how counterpart policies respond. The task prompt must state the rules needed to make the decision while preserving the intended private information. These choices define the mechanism that a score is meant to test.

The author then provides a world generator or a fixed evaluation set, an inclusion criterion, and a reference whose access to information is explicit. The inclusion criterion screens each candidate world before any agent plays and before hidden types are drawn. It is computed exactly from the public world and the stated prior. It keeps a world only if simple shortcuts, such as accepting the default terms or ignoring private information, fall short of the reference by a stated margin. Whether the reference can be reached is a separate check. Executable controls play the reference through the actual environment and verifier on every included world. Simple policies are played beside it, to reveal shortcuts or ceiling effects that the screen missed. The evaluation set must also be valid, free of duplicates, and split into disjoint development and holdout ranges. A full-information bound is labelled separately from a policy the evaluated seat could execute. The case states its score units, the direction of each score, and its treatment of invalid actions and incomplete episodes.

Before a confirmatory comparison, the campaign freezes the worlds, seat assignments, prompts, agent settings, budgets and analysis, and pre-registers them. Its evidence package should connect each reported measure to the retained actions and outcomes, the scoring implementation, and a command that reproduces it. Section 4 describes the shared execution path. The current drivers and headline analyses extend beyond what a receipt covers in places, which Appendix H records.

This is the contribution and qualification standard. The four examples meet it to different degrees: their implementation support and completed checks vary (Section 6). Cases can be useful for development before qualification, provided their reports identify missing checks, preserve failed attempts, and distinguish analyses pre-registered before execution from later corrections. The current examples demonstrate the workflow and its failure modes; they do not establish successful adoption by external contributors.

### 4.1 Verification

A check compares an action or an outcome with a reference and reports a pass or fail, a count or an amount. The cases use four kinds of reference. Refund uses an answer key, which specifies the correct resolution of each claim. Procurement and the agreement case also check rules, which constrain what an action may do. Housing, Procurement and the data-center case measure value, comparing an economic outcome with a policy or a bound. The data-center case also compares named agents with each other. Results keep their native units, and we report no cross-family aggregate. A value reference is one of three types: a policy the evaluated seat could carry out, the best policy given the deciding seat’s information, or a full-information upper bound that the seat need not be able to reach.

The case-study scores are computed by code. Kernel replay reconstructs the state and each receipt’s score from the recorded actions, without calling a model. Replay therefore establishes that a score agrees with the recorded execution. Whether the score measures the intended decision is a separate question, which the quality-control checks of Section <a href="#sec:qc" data-reference="sec:qc" data-reference-type="ref">6</a> address. Housing and Procurement also report headline measures that are computed outside the receipt and verified separately; Appendix <a href="#app:runtime-cases" data-reference="app:runtime-cases" data-reference-type="ref">H.3</a> documents how each is computed.

### 4.2 Evaluation harness

Before execution, the kernel hashes a plan that specifies the worlds, seat assignments, agent configurations, seeds and budgets. During simultaneous play, it freezes all acting seats’ observations before requesting actions and then applies their joint transition. Calls through its provider and tool interfaces are logged with typed outcomes. Finalization seals the evidence and produces a receipt binding the plan, world and scores.

A seat is played in one of three ways. Most models run through AERead’s own harness. Each action is a single call through a pinned provider route. The call receives the seat’s instructions and its current observation, and it returns one action in the case’s schema. It has no tools and no memory beyond what the observation shows. The harness checks the route against its pin, requests a seed, and records the specified reasoning setting and output limit. Gemini 3.8 Flash, GLM 5.3 Flash and GPT-6 Luna in Housing (Gemini and Luna in Procurement), the Refund models and the TERMS-Bench agents are run this way.

The two frontier agents, Opus 5.5 and Sol 6.1, run instead through their vendors’ command-line tools, Claude Code and Codex. Each action starts a fresh process at low reasoning effort, with the same instructions and schema, tools disabled or refused, and no session carried over. Neither tool was fully isolated from the operator’s machine. Claude Code ran in safe mode, with none of the operator’s instructions, memory or skills, but its working directory was in its context. Codex still listed eight of the operator’s own skills (Table <a href="#tab:cli-controls" data-reference="tab:cli-controls" data-reference-type="ref">H.1</a>). Only the tool’s version and binary are checked, and the tool exposes no seed, temperature or output limit. Through the harness, the system under evaluation is the model; through a command-line tool, it is the product, including the tool’s own instructions and internal turns. A comparison across the two paths therefore compares agent configurations rather than models, and each result names its path.

The third way is a script. Counterparts, references and simple-rule baselines are scripted, call no model and replay exactly.

A recorded setting does not establish that a provider honoured it. Appendix <a href="#app:runtime" data-reference="app:runtime" data-reference-type="ref">H</a> gives the controls each path applied and the versions behind each reported panel.

## 5. Case studies

We present four cases (Table 5.1). Each is a starting point for questions about the decision it models, and Section 7.2 lists the questions we consider open. The cases show how the counterpart changes measured value and how a shortfall divides into typed action classes, and Section 5.3 runs an external benchmark on the same runtime. We describe Housing, Procurement and Data center here and specify them in Appendices A–D; Refund appears only in the cross-case comparison. Each case is a family rather than a fixed task set. It consists of a generator of worlds; a case-specific reference, which is either a policy that uses only what the deciding side may know or an explicitly labelled full-information upper bound; an inclusion criterion that keeps the worlds in which the decision matters (its form varies by case; Appendix E.2); and a set of verifiers. The references differ in what a shortfall means. Against a reachable policy, the shortfall is the value that a feasible strategy would have kept. Against the best policy given the decider's information, it is the whole cost of the decisions. Against a full-information bound, it also includes the value of information that no seat had (Appendix C).

We choose cases by the economic value of the decision rather than by occupation (Li 2026). An activity qualifies when its value is decided rather than produced, when a counterparty has its own interest and private information, and when the result can be scored within a world against a computed reference. The four examples lie in two of the five transaction classes introduced in §3, business and household purchases. We chose them to span the kinds of verifier and counterpart, not to sample a representative distribution.

### 5.1 Results

The four cases are worked examples that we built and maintain. Their results therefore compare the named agents on these worlds; they are not qualified benchmark scores. Each result is also bounded by its case's quality-control gates, which Section 6 describes and Table 6.1 summarises. Below, "pre-registered" describes how a comparison was run: its analysis was committed to the repository before any episode.

**Housing.** The structure follows informal rental markets on social platforms such as RedNote. Listings are posted with photos that a tenant cannot check before viewing, a viewing costs time, and good listings are often taken while the tenant decides. We set the rents, the lemon share and the inspection cost ourselves; they are not drawn from platform data. A tenant chooses among four listings, two of which are lemons. It knows its own value v<sub>ℓ</sub> for each listing if sound, but not which two are lemons; each of the six pairs is equally likely. In each of three rounds it may pay \$25 to inspect one listing, make one offer, and sign or walk away from a lease it is offered, while each listing it did not bid on is taken by outside demand with probability ½. Signing listing ℓ at rent x after k inspections pays

<span class="eq-body">v<sub>ℓ</sub> − 1,000·𝟙\[ℓ is a lemon\] − x − 25k,</span><span class="eq-number">(1)</span>

and signing nothing pays −25k. The difficulty lies in information. A listing worth \$400 more than its ask if sound is worth 400 − ½·1,000 = −\$100 if signed blind at the ask, but ½·400 − 25 = +\$175 if it is inspected first and signed only when sound; inspecting a second listing takes another round, in which each listing not bid on may be taken. A landlord's reply can reveal a lemon at no cost: every world is played against one landlord kind whose reply to a low offer reveals a lemon and one whose reply does not. We score a blind signing at its expected value given what the tenant knew, so that the score follows the decision rather than the draw, with the odds conditioned on every informative landlord reply. This paper introduces the corrected measure. The comparison reported here, v15, was pre-registered before any of its artifacts. Each included world is screened so that the best informed tenant beats a simple inspect-then-sign rule by at least \$25, and the tenant's task prompt states how landlords price. A holdout of 40 worlds changes one mechanism, outside demand at 0.3 a round, and its task prompt says so.

**Procurement.** The structure follows wholesale sourcing on B2B platforms such as Alibaba's 1688. A displayed price is not a quote, a listing's quality is known only after it is checked, and new sellers of one kind often buy from the same factory. A buyer owes 50 kits by a deadline and awards the order to one supplier: either a recorded supplier with a long clean record or one of three newcomers. Each newcomer is good, late or poor. The buyer knows only the odds, which are read from the record of the newcomer's seller kind, and that newcomers of one kind share a source with a stated probability (0.5 to 0.95), in which case they are all of the same type. Two features follow our own sourcing on 1688. A newcomer's price relative to the recorded supplier's is drawn from the price gaps in that record, so a newcomer is dearer about half the time, and a newcomer answers a quote request only a third of the time. We set the case's other quantities ourselves. Among up to ten actions the buyer may request a quote (\$2 and a day; an answered quote exposes a late supplier), request a sample (\$20 to \$240 and three days; it always arrives and exposes a poor supplier), look up a listing's registered factory (\$1; two lookups in one kind show whether the kind shares a source), award the order, or defer. An award that delivers k kits on time earns

<span class="eq-body">37k − 12(50 − k) − cost of the order − cost of the information bought,</span><span class="eq-number">(2)</span>

and an award that delivers fewer than 40 kits, as a late or poor supplier does, earns −\$600 less the information bought (Appendix A.2).

The difficulty is a test worth taking only for what it reveals about another supplier. Every world in the evaluation set is one in which a rule that plans a single newcomer's test exactly (what to request first, how often to repeat an unanswered quote, and when to give up) pays the premium, while the best policy tests a newcomer first and beats paying the premium by at least 10%. In one world the recorded supplier asks \$24.95. A newcomer asks \$12.47, but its sample costs \$200 and its odds are 0.20 good, 0.15 late and 0.65 poor, so testing it on its own loses money. A second newcomer of the same seller kind asks \$26.87, more than the recorded supplier, so it is never worth buying; but its sample costs \$30, and the two share a source with probability 0.95. Once the cheap newcomer's quote has come back clean, a sound sample of the dear newcomer raises the cheap newcomer's probability of being good to 0.94, and a poor sample lowers it to 0.01. The best policy therefore requests a quote from the cheap newcomer; if the quote comes back clean, it samples the dear newcomer, looks both up, and only then pays for the \$200 sample. It earns 11.8% more than paying the premium. Every action is charged the value it gave up relative to the exact best informed policy at that point.

**Further runs.** A development pilot and four diagnostics on this evaluation set, among them a frozen control, leave this picture unchanged (Appendix E.4, Table E.15). One of them bears on why Opus never tests. Asked, with no play, how a sample of one newcomer changes the odds of another of its kind, Opus answered all 18 questions within 0.05 of the exact value, as Sol did. Opus therefore applies the sharing rule when asked, but does not plan with it. **Data center.** The structure follows a real AI-infrastructure engagement in which a system integrator bundles hardware, data-center space, staffing and software into a working system for a client. Hardware can arrive late or defective, the site may not be ready, and the contract's warranty, delay-damages, liability-cap, site-readiness, deposit and burn-in clauses decide who bears each cost. We set the costs, probabilities and prices ourselves. Client and integrator negotiate one agreement, consisting of a position on each of the eight clauses (600 distinct agreements) and an all-in price p. Each side knows its own cost of carrying risk and only the probability distribution of the other's: the integrator's pre-staging cost and risk charge, and the client's risk charge. Over six alternating moves, a side may redline any clauses and name a price, sign the standing version, or walk away, and each redline after the first move costs \$25k. In thousands of dollars and net of redline fees, a signed agreement c pays the integrator and the client, respectively,

<span class="eq-body">π<sub>I</sub> = p − C<sub>I</sub>(c) − 500, π<sub>C</sub> = O<sub>C</sub> − p − C<sub>C</sub>(c),</span><span class="eq-number">(3)</span>

where 500 is the integrator's outside option, O<sub>C</sub> the client's, and C<sub>I</sub> and C<sub>C</sub> each side's expected cost of the risks that the clauses leave with it, marked up by its risk charge; no deal pays only the fees. The difficulty is that price only divides the value, while the clauses determine how much value there is, and the best clauses depend on what the other side hides. Burn-in, for example, costs the integrator \$40k and, in one world, costs the client a week's delay worth \$110k, and it halves the probability of a \$3 million incident that the client carries. It therefore lowers joint cost by \$41k for a client with the highest risk charge and raises it by \$19k or \$64k for the other two, and the integrator cannot tell which client it faces. The comparison reported here (v2) was pre-registered before its runs, on 80 included worlds (from 5,436 scanned), 72 of them with a mutually beneficial deal. Both command-line tools were isolated, the client's task prompt states its walk-away point, and a world is included only if the integrator has at least \$25k of room over a price-only rule.

The labels name what happened. The mechanism behind each large segment is as follows.

**Procurement: three models never test, and Sol secures the recorded supplier first.** Opus, Gemini and Luna asked no newcomer for anything in any of their 120 plays: they requested quotes and samples from the recorded supplier and awarded it the order, so nearly all of their loss is information not acquired, and the rest is award checks, each of which spends an action. Luna's further \$6.85 a world comes from ordering 50 units where 55 was best in 15 of its 40 plays. The pre-registered classes file an award's whole loss under information not acquired when the award came before a worthwhile test, so \$6.33 a world of that worse-quantity loss sits in Luna's information-not-acquired segment (Table E.10). Sol's loss divides by world. Where it never tested a newcomer that the best policy would have tested, it lost \$17.5 a world. Where it did test, it often first requested a quote and a sample from the recorded supplier, spending days and actions before the newcomer test that the stated odds and clock made best (\$8.9 a world), or it awarded the order while a newcomer test it had begun was still worth finishing (\$3.2).

**Housing, Gemini: it drops a known lemon even where the lemon is the better lease.** Worse terms account for 80% of its shortfall relative to the reachable rule (\$40.7 of \$51.1 a world), and nearly all of it comes from one move. Under the landlord kind that prices a lemon from its own lower cost, a lemon's landlord counters about \$1,000 below a sound listing's floor (\$995 to \$1,000 in these worlds), so a known lemon signed at that counter is worth what the listing would be worth sound at a sound floor. This holds exactly in 187 of the 200 worlds whose favourite is a lemon, and within \$5 in the rest. The rule inspects the favourite, finds the lemon, offers \$0 and signs the counter in all 200. Gemini also inspected the favourite and found the lemon in all 200, but made an offer on it in only 4; in 172 episodes it leased a sound listing that left it with less (\$65.0 a world). Under the other landlord kind the rule leases nothing in 77 worlds, and Gemini's leases there offset \$26.5 a world. Gemini does read landlords' replies: of 74 replies that proved a lemon, it walked away from 64 and signed above the lemon's value once. Ending with no lease where the rule leased costs a further \$8.9 (12 of 520 episodes).

**Housing, Luna: it drops the lemons worth taking and signs ones that cost it.** Its shortfall relative to the reachable rule is \$99.9 a world. Worse terms (54%, \$54.3) follow Gemini's pattern: in the same 200 worlds Luna found the lemon favourite in 197 and made an offer on it in 7, and in 154 episodes it leased a sound listing that left it with less (\$57.7 a world). Signing a listing that a landlord's reply had proved a lemon accounts for 22% (\$22.2, 11 episodes): the landlord accepted Luna's offer at a rent that no sound landlord would take, which by the stated pricing rule proves a lemon, and Luna signed at that rent, above the lemon's value. Of 89 such replies, it walked away from 58 and signed 20 at or below the lemon's value. Another 15% (\$14.9, 64 episodes) comes from signing without settling quality under the landlord kind whose replies reveal nothing, where an inspection was worth its \$25 fee every time; 21 of those listings were lemons. Ending with no lease where the rule leased costs \$8.6. In v14, whose task prompt did not state how landlords price, the reply-proved lemon accounted for 60% of Luna's shortfall.

**Housing, Sol and Opus (addendum B): what remains is the price.** Neither lost anything by acting against what it knew, and neither left a worthwhile inspection undone, apart from Opus's \$1.4 a world. Worse terms account for all of Sol's \$15.3 shortfall (\$15.7, offset by \$0.4 of inspection fees it saved) and for 84% of Opus's \$46.1 (\$38.8); ending with no lease where the rule leased costs Opus a further \$6.3.

**Refund, Gemini 2.5 Flash Lite: the case ends before it decides.** In 81% of its episodes it asked again for a fact it had already been given; the environment rejects a repeated request, and the case ends with no decision (80.8 of its 98.3 points). In most of the remaining episodes it denied the claim correctly but wrote the refund method as null where the policy's format requires "none" (16.7 points). It reached the answer key's outcome in 18% of episodes and a wrong outcome in none.

**Data center: fees, then deals the client walked away from.** Every redline after the opening move costs \$25k. The full-information reference signs the best agreement at once, so each fee counts as loss, whichever side paid it. Against the firm counterparts, across all 80 worlds with both seats averaged, fees account for 59% of Sol's joint loss (\$83.4k of \$140.2k a world) and 54% of Opus's (\$45.3k of \$83.6k). Sol redlines more: as integrator it made 371 redlines to the firm client's 262, whereas Opus made 227 to 189. Some of those redlines buy better clauses, and a seat that does not know the other side's costs may need them, so their share alone does not show wasted negotiation. Nearly all of the remaining loss is deals lost in worlds that had one (35% of Sol's loss and 34% of Opus's), and every one of them ended with the client walking away. With each model as integrator, the firm client walked away from Sol in 40 of its 144 plays of deal worlds and from Opus in 14. As client, each model walked away in 26 of the 72 deal worlds. No agreement was harmful: with the walk-away point in the task prompt, neither model signed in any of the 8 worlds without a deal.

The pre-registered plan included a replay of the recorded moves at other fees, which shows what the same moves would earn, not how the models would play. Without fees, Sol still loses more joint value than Opus against the firm counterparts (\$63.1k against \$42.5k a world, seats averaged) and earns more in both seats. At \$50k a redline its earnings fall below Opus's (as integrator 67.9 against 69.7, as client 1.3 against 22.8, in thousands of dollars per deal world), because it redlines more. Appendix E.9 gives v1's fuller replay.

**Across the cases.** Four findings hold across the cases. First, the gap lies in a stratum that the design fixes in advance, so every case reports by stratum and pools last. Second, the counterpart is part of the measurement, so a claim names the counterparts it covers. Third, what the task prompt states moves the score as much as the subject does (stating two rules cut procurement loss by \$11 to \$14; stating a walk-away cost cut losing signatures from 15 of 60 to 1 of 60), so the task prompt is a versioned part of the case. Fourth, a pattern found by looking back is a hypothesis until a pre-registered test confirms it.

### 5.3 An external benchmark on the same runtime

TERMS-Bench evaluates bilateral price negotiation against counterparts whose type is hidden (Zhang et al. 2026). We reimplemented, from the paper's description, its alternating-offer environment, three of its six counterpart families (Candid, Taciturn and Expressive) and four of its measures as one AERead family. The counterpart runs as a scripted seat, and its random draws are sealed each turn, so replay recomputes the counterpart's moves as well as the scores. We did not implement the Oracle-Cue reference or the gap measures built on it, the urgency regime, type-belief error or the other three families, and counterpart messages are templates. The corpus has 30 cases, 15 with a zone of agreement and 15 without, each played once.

The paper states how much the baselines concede (1%, 10% or 30% of the remaining distance per offer) but not how they open or accept. No agent has played the reproduction. The paper reports critical violations by GLM-5.1 in 5% of cases without a zone of agreement for these three families; at that rate, 15 such cases would show none about half the time, and ours showed none. This is why the reasoning condition is part of each run's frozen configuration. The runtime's support for the scripted seat that this family uses is now on the main branch.

## 6. Quality control

The defects that mattered in the four cases were properties of the distribution, the task prompt, the counterpart or the analysis, and every world was individually correct when they were found. In one, a seat-number tie-break decided who signed the good listing; in another, a prompt pointed every agent at the right supplier in half the worlds and at the wrong one in the other half; in a third, a hidden type was tied to the stratum of the evaluation set, so that comparisons by type compared worlds. An inspection of one world at a time passes all three. We therefore organise quality control around populations of worlds, pre-registered analyses and recorded failures. Appendix F describes each check that we applied to these cases, and only those.

**Gates.** A case is qualified through six quality-control gates, whose verdicts are recorded in its family's QC profile. Gate 0 requires that the profile exists; Gate 1, that the worlds are valid, distinct, split and leave headroom; Gate 2, that actions change the intended state and that every score is rebuilt from sealed evidence; Gate 3, that a better-informed policy does better and that shortcuts lose; Gate 4, that the result supports the attribution it claims; and Gate 5, that the comparison was sized, frozen and executed without tuning on outcomes. Each gate has one of five statuses (`passed`, `failed`, `partial`, `not_run` or `not_applicable`), and only `passed` qualifies a result. We wrote the profiles after the initial runs. The confirmatory run of the agreement case came after its profile. Housing and Procurement pass four of Gates 1 to 5, all except Gate 4, and no case yet passes all five (Table 6.1).

**Headroom (Gate 1).** When a case computes its reference exactly, as Housing and Procurement do, every shortcut named in its profile must lose a pre-specified margin to the reference in every world. Otherwise, a frozen model control screens the worlds. We added the first route on 21 October (Appendix F.1). The data-center agreement's reference is a lower bound, and its evaluation set is frozen, so its Gate 1 can pass only on a new evaluation set.

**The reference is played on every world.** For each case, a test plays the reference through the real environment and verifier on every included world, with no model, and checks that it reaches its stated result. Every reported evaluation set has such a test; Appendix F.2 lists them, including the ones we wrote after the runs they check. Two of the tests, the data-center grade and the Housing ceiling, are also shown to fail when the grade or the reference is wrong.

**Other checks.** Every committed evaluation set is regenerated byte for byte from its seeds, and the Housing v14 and v15 receipts replay from their sealed events. We report trivial agents beside the evaluated agents, score Procurement's fixed rules exactly, and audited the task prompts for instructions that decide the outcome. Uncertainty is computed over worlds, and every claim names its counterparts. Six reported analyses were pre-registered: Housing v14 and v15 (with v15's two addenda), Procurement's main test, the data-center signalling test and the two confirmatory runs of the agreement case. Procurement's frozen-control diagnostic was also pre-registered before its plays.

**What it found.** We record every discovered failure in an incident log, as design, operational, tooling or judgment rows that are never deleted. On 16 October the log held 51 entries about Housing, 57 about Procurement, 90 about the data center and 23 about Refund (Table F.3), and rows have been added since. Of the 25 defects selected for Table F.4, 14 were invisible in any single world. We found the 11 visible ones by reading code or sealed bytes, by tracing a case, or within a single gate. Nine defects were open when we compiled the table. A later reanalysis from the sealed actions resolves one of them for the reported panels, which leaves eight open. The same checks corrected three of our own statements while we wrote this paper.

## 7. Discussion and conclusion

### 7.1 What the cases show

AERead evaluates an agent’s economic decisions by the value it obtains for a principal, given the information, resources and authority of its seat. Each score is measured against a reference whose access to information is stated. It keeps its own units and names the counterparts it was measured against, so every shortfall has a defined interpretation. Our cases apply this to information acquisition, bargaining and commitment in housing, procurement and contracting, and a reimplementation of part of TERMS-Bench runs on the same runtime.

The cases teach three lessons that bear on any evaluation of this kind. The counterpart is part of the measurement: the same two agents differ by far more when they negotiate with each other than when each faces a fixed counterpart. The task prompt is part of the case: stating a rule that the prompt had left implicit changed scores as much as changing the agent did. And the defects that matter are often properties of the population of worlds, the task prompt or the analysis rather than of any single world. Construction and quality control therefore belong in the method, and the checks of Section 6 changed the interpretation of our own results. Loss categories locate a shortfall but do not explain it, and explaining it requires controlled follow-up comparisons. Replay establishes consistency with recorded play; construct validity requires evidence about the worlds, the information and the controls.

These properties matter most where agents are deployed. Organizations that pilot agents often stop because the business value is unclear and the risks are hard to control (Gartner 2025); a value score against a stated reference and a typed account of losses address both. Small firms face a different problem: short of staff (National Federation of Independent Business 2026), they often leave decisions like those our cases model to one person, without specialized expertise and with no review before a commitment. Delegating such a decision to an agent is attractive there, and trusting it requires evidence that the agent keeps value against the counterparts it will meet.

The present evidence has clear limits. The cases take their decision structure from informal rentals, wholesale sourcing and AI-infrastructure contracting, but we set their parameters; calibration to field data and transfer to field settings remain to be established. Coverage is limited to two transaction classes and a small set of agent configurations, with no human policy baseline. No case passes all of the quality-control gates (Section 6). Several checks were added after the runs they qualify. The behavior of hosted models and command-line tools also limits how far a result can be attributed to its stated settings. Versioned reanalyses now regenerate the corrected Housing measures and Procurement decision losses from sealed records, which strengthens their provenance. Full verification against the original execution still requires the retained logs (Appendix G). The reanalyses neither qualify the campaigns retroactively nor resolve Housing’s historical seat attribution (Appendix H). The results therefore do not establish general model rankings or deployment readiness.

### 7.2 Open research directions and contributions

AERead is shared infrastructure for studying how agents make economic decisions. Its code is open source under the Apache-2.0 license (github.com/aeread-org/AERead; contact@aeread.org), and contributors can pursue their own questions with the existing cases, counterparts and evidence. The results above leave four questions open, each suited to a different kind of contribution.

- **Counterpart populations.** In the Data-center case, the gap between two agents depended strongly on whether they negotiated against each other or each faced a fixed script. How far does an agent’s measured skill depend on whom it faces, can rankings reverse across counterpart populations (none did in our runs), and which populations separate distinct strategic capabilities? The scripted styles, head-to-head pairings and counterpart seats are in place.

- **Validity of populations of worlds.** More than half of the defects we selected from our record were invisible in any single world. Which checks find such defects before a campaign runs, and what should qualify a case? The quality-control gates, the incident record and the failed campaigns are available for study.

- **Information and judgment.** Stating a rule that the task prompt had left implicit changed scores as much as changing the agent. Separating what an agent was told from how it decided, and computing best-informed references for richer cases, are open methodological problems.

- **New economic decisions.** A case for another activity supplies its rules, worlds and reference; execution, replay, counterpart seats and the quality-control standard come with the runtime.

Some components are ready and others are not. The runtime, its sealed evidence and replay, and the case layout are in use, and Housing, Procurement and Data center each have a world generator, references and scripted counterparts. No case is yet fully qualified. Section <a href="#sec:qc" data-reference="sec:qc" data-reference-type="ref">6</a> gives every gate verdict, and Appendix F lists each campaign’s open checks, so a contributor can see what a case still needs before its results can rank agents.

The next steps follow from the limits in Section 7.1. Future campaigns should bind the actual seat assignments and headline scorers into frozen evaluation configurations and run the required controls before any comparison, and the TERMS-Bench reimplementation needs the protocol fixes described in Section 5.3 before a pre-registered reproduction. Practitioner review would calibrate the cases’ parameters, human baselines would show where agents stand against people, and externally contributed cases would test whether the structure serves authors other than its maintainers. AERead’s present contribution is that structure, a common way to construct evaluations of economic decisions, together with an evidence-based account of the checks needed to interpret what they measure.
